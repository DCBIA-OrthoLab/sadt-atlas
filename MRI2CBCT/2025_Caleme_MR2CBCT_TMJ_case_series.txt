==== ARTICLE-TITLE ====
Aligning MRI and CBCT for Advanced TMJ Diagnostics: Case Series Using
AI-Powered Registration in Dentistry and Orthodontics

==== ABSTRACT ====
This study demonstrates the functionality and clinical value of magnetic
resonance imaging (MRI) to cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) registration
using a new open-source artificial intelligence (AI) model called MR2CBCT. We
present five clinical cases in which the AI-based method was used to register
CBCT and MRI images. For comparison, manual registration was also performed.
Qualitative inspection revealed that manual alignment often showed errors that
could compromise diagnostic accuracy. In contrast, the AI-based approach
consistently corrected these discrepancies, producing more anatomically coherent
fused images to better support clinical decision-making. Our findings highlight
MR2CBCT as a reliable and accessible tool for multimodal integration in
temporomandibular joint (TMJ) assessment in orthodontics and general
dentistry.

==== BODY ====
Introduction The advent of three-dimensional imaging has brought profound advancements to
both medicine and dentistry. Among the most impactful technologies are Cone Beam
Computed Tomography (CBCT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), each offering
detailed visualization of anatomical structures from complementary perspectives.
CBCT is particularly effective for assessing hard tissues and has become
increasingly utilized in dentistry over the past decades, especially for pre- and
post-surgical evaluations by oral and maxillofacial surgeons 1 – 2 . More recently, its diagnostic potential has also been
recognized in orthodontics 2 – 3 , where enhanced spatial accuracy can
improve treatment planning and outcome assessment. MRI, by contrast, provides high-resolution imaging of soft tissues 2 , 4 – 7 , While it
allows for the visualization of hard tissue, this can prove challenging to
clinicians. MRIs have been predominantly used in medical contexts. However, its
relevance in dental practice has grown significantly, particularly in the management
of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders, where soft tissue integrity plays a
critical role in diagnosis 6 . Despite the distinct advantages of each modality, no single imaging technique
currently enables simultaneous three-dimensional visualization of both hard and soft
tissues in a clinically convenient form. This limitation is clinically significant
in conditions such as TMJ degeneration, where pathological changes may affect both
the bony structures of the mandibular condyle and the soft tissues of the articular
disc, since it proves extremely challenging for clinicians to fully capture these
interrelated changes by analyzing only one of these image modalities, multimodal
registration becomes a valuable diagnostic tool 6 . To address this, researchers have proposed multimodal image integration,
wherein CBCT and MRI scans of the same patient are superimposed using a stable
anatomical reference 9 – 12 . While conceptually
straightforward, this process is technically demanding: differences in head
positioning, imaging geometry, and the complexity of MRI interpretation make
accurate registration difficult for most clinicians. It is important to note the many challenges when integrating two different
imaging modalities, particularly, voxel and slice sizes and difficulty with
different fields of view (FOV) between these modalities can make registration
particularly challenging. Differences in voxel size and slice thickness are major
complications 8 since
voxel-based registration Is one of the most reliable ways to superimpose an image,
recently these issues have been circumvented by utilizing robust registration
models, these are models that rely on intensity independent methods like Mutual
information instead of merely trying to match voxel for voxel, one of the prime
models utilized is Elastix 9 , 13 . So far, there is a limited number of studies that address the use of MRI and
CBCT integration in clinical research 9 – 12 , some
suggesting good accuracy for superimposition while others remain skeptical. This is
expected, since only relatively recently robust registration models have become
available to address the issues of voxel size, slice thickness, and other factors.
But now that this integration is possible, it becomes fundamental for clinicians and
researchers to consider utilizing this new method of analysis Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a powerful ally in overcoming
these challenges by automating parts of the image registration process. Recent
developments from our group include a novel open-source multimodal registration
module integrated into the 3D Slicer software platform named MR2CBCT, described in
detail in this study. It offers clinicians an accessible way to fuse CBCT and MRI
data, potentially enabling more comprehensive diagnostics and advancing
individualized standards of care in orthodontics and beyond 9 . Therefore, this study aims to present the clinical application using 5 cases
of a recently developed open-source artificial intelligence tool to assist
clinicians, called MR2CBT. Materials and Methods This pilot study aimed to present a novel multimodal image registration tool
(MR2CBCT) recently developed by our research team using artificial intelligence
algorithms on an open-source platform called 3D-slicer 14 . The study was granted exemption by the
University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (IRB HUM00239207), and the
requirement for informed consent was waived. The MR2CBCT tool was developed by the Dental and Craniofacial Bionetwork for
Image Analysis (DCBIA). Technical details regarding its development can be found at
 9 . This paper will showcase
five cases to better understand what clinicians can achieve with this tool. Figure
one shows a small guide with images from the 3Dd Slicer software to assist
clinicians with utilizing this tool. Imaging Protocol and User Guide The software 3D slicer 14 , version 5.9.0 ( https://download.slicer.org/ ) was used for all of the processing
steps cited. The steps for using the tool MR2CBCT are as follows: I) Imaging Pre-Processing Before utilizing the MR2CBCT module, clinicians must ensure that the
“Automated Dental Tools” extension is properly installed, as
MR2CBCT is integrated within that suite. Next, both the CBCT and MRI scans
must be saved in NIfTI format. II) CBCT orientation and segmentation The CBCT should be oriented and segmented before registration. This
can be done using the commands in the “Pre-Processing” section
of the MR2CBCT module ( Figure 1 ) or
through other tools within the Automated Dental Tools extension 15 . For orientation, the ASO
module is recommended, while segmentation can be performed using either the
AMASS or BatchSegmentator models. III) MRI pre-processing and image cropping For MRI pre-processing, clinicians should use the section
immediately below the CBCT pre-processing area ( Figure 2 ), which allows for MRI orientation and
centering. While manual adjustment of the orientation guidelines is
possible, the default settings provide reliable alignment in most cases. It
is essential to crop both the MRI and CBCT scans to focus on the
temporomandibular joint (TMJ) region, as this is the primary area of
interest for the model. Cropping can be done using the command box located
below the MRI pre-processing area ( Figure
3 ). Alternatively, clinicians may use the “Auto Crop
3D” module from the Automated Dental Tools extension, which allows
them to define a custom bounding box for image cropping. IV) Resampling With the oriented and segmented images, clinicians can proceede to
the resampling step, which is fundamental for making sure both the CBCT and
MRI have the same parameters, which makes registration possible. The
“resampling” command box is the one above all other boxes
( Figure 4 ) V) Approximation Once all pre-processing steps are complete, the clinician can
proceed with image registration. The first step is to perform an initial
approximation, found below the pre-processing section ( Figure 5 ). The clinician must select the folders
containing the cropped and oriented MRI and CBCT scans, then click the
“Approximate” button. This step helps align the two datasets
by correcting large angular and linear discrepancies, making accurate
registration possible. VÎ) Final Registration Following approximation, the clinician should move to the command
box below the approximation step ( Figure
6 ). Here, they must select the folders containing the MRI, CBCT,
and the previously obtained CBCT segmentation. The clinician can then
configure the normalization parameters, a critical step that ensures the
intensity profiles of both scans are compatible for registration. While
customization is possible, the two default normalization options are
generally suitable for most cases. When all steps are completed, the
registered image is saved in the output folder specified by the
clinician. Registration Based on Artificial Intelligence and Imaging Processing The parameters and protocols employed by our AI model are
comprehensively described by Leroux et al. 9 This model provides users with an automated pipeline
that integrates AI algorithms with traditional computational methods. Artificial
intelligence is utilized by this model to perform image segmentation and
orientation tasks. These components are essential, as both orientation and
segmentation present significant challenges and are time-intensive processes,
particularly for clinicians with limited expertise in medical image
interpretation and minimal experience with segmentation software
applications. Additional pipeline components, including centering and resampling
(geometric transformations), image cropping, MRI gray-level inversion, and
intensity normalization, constitute mathematical, geometrical, and statistical
operations. The registration process itself is implemented using
Elastix 13 , with core
registration procedures such as mutual information calculation, rigid
registration optimization, iterative parameter adjustment, and mask application
representing a combination of geometrical, mathematical, and statistical
computations. Nevertheless, all operations performed by this module whether AI-based
or traditional computational methods, are imperative for achieving accurate
multimodal registration. This hybrid approach leverages the pattern recognition
capabilities of artificial intelligence for complex perceptual tasks while
employing established mathematical frameworks for geometric computations,
thereby ensuring both clinical usability and registration precision. Registration Validation Quantitative assessment The present case series considered both quantitative and qualitative
criteria to assess registration quality. Quantitatively, the AI-based model
has been previously validated in the literature 9 , where it demonstrated improved
linear and angular alignment metrics compared to manual registration
performed by clinical experts using multimodal datasets. This prior
validation supports the technical accuracy of the tool used in our case
series. Qualitative Assessment Qualitatively, the registrations were evaluated through a structured
consensus process among clinical experts based on specific anatomical
criteria. Each case was initially assessed by an experienced clinician and
subsequently reviewed by additional clinical experts until consensus was
reached on registration quality. In the sagittal superimpositions, the
evaluators analyzed the relationship between the articular disc, condyle,
and glenoid fossa, focusing on potential interpositions or misalignments and
how well the disc accommodated within the joint space. In the 3D
segmentations, emphasis was placed on coronal and axial alignment (roll and
yaw) and the presence of overlapping structures. In the volumetric images,
attention was given to the visual congruence of the mandibular condyle with
adjacent anatomical landmarks such as the zygomatic arch, coronoid process,
and temporal bone. This combined framework enabled a structured clinical
interpretation of registration quality while relying on previously published
quantitative benchmarks of the AI-based tool. Results and Discussion In the five cases presented (Figures
7-11), we demonstrate the
superimposition of MRI and CBCT images focused on the temporomandibular joint (TMJ)
region. This multimodal fusion allows clinicians to visualize various TMJ structures
more accurately and comprehensively. For example, condylar resorption is more
clearly observed in the CBCT scan, as shown in Figure
7. While this finding may be technically visible on the MRI, it would be
challenging for most clinicians to identify with confidence. In contrast, the CBCT
provides a more distinct view of bony morphology, making the small and altered shape
of the condyle readily apparent. Disk abnormalities, on the other hand, are more effectively assessed using
MRI due to its superior soft tissue contrast. Abnormalities in the glenoid fossa are
best visualized with CBCT. Key spatial relationships such as those between the
articular disk, the condyle, and the fossa require input from both modalities to
achieve optimal diagnostic accuracy. In all five cases, patients exhibited
morphological or positional abnormalities involving the condyle, articular disk,
and/or glenoid fossa. While it may be technically possible to evaluate some of these
features using a single imaging modality, doing so would be extremely challenging
for most clinicians. This highlights the clinical value and practicality of the
MR2CBCT tool in supporting precise, comprehensive TMJ evaluations. CASE ANALYSIS CASE I In the first case ( Figure 7 ), we
compare the registration outcomes produced by a clinician expert (Panels A-F)
and our AI model (Panels G-L). In the grayscale superimpositions (Panels C and
H), the articular disk appears slightly more anteriorly positioned in the manual
registration (Panel C), while in the AI registration (Panel H), the disk aligns
more naturally between the condyle and glenoid fossa. This improved anatomical
congruency suggests a more accurate positioning of soft tissue structures by the
AI. When analyzing the segmented models (Panels D and I), the disk in the
manual registration seems anteriorly displaced and not properly engaged with the
condyle appearing almost to “float” within the joint space. In
contrast, the AI registration shows the disk centered and in functional contact
with both the condyle and fossa. These differences cannot be fully attributed to
segmentation variability alone. In the 3D volume renderings (Panels E-F and K-L), the manual
registration is more difficult to interpret due to the lack of resampling, and
the cranial structures reveal a slight upward pitch of the MRI relative to the
CT. Meanwhile, the AI registration benefits from automated resampling, allowing
better visualization and demonstrating superior alignment, note the better fit
on the condyle and coronoid process. Overall, this case illustrates subtle but clinically significant
differences between manual and AI-assisted registration, favoring the
model’s consistency and anatomical fidelity in the latter. Patient 1 presented with frequent headaches and earaches following a
single incident in which she opened her mouth and heard a cracking sound in the
temporomandibular joint (TMJ). An initial attempt at treatment using a
stabilization splint worsened her symptoms. Clinically, she presented with a
full dentition, midline drift in both anterior and posterior teeth, Class II
Division II malocclusion, limited mouth opening, restricted mandibular
translation and rotation, and pain in the face, TMJ, and shoulders. MRI analysis performed by a radiologist revealed signs of arthritic
changes in the condyle and raised suspicions of anterior and medial displacement
of the articular disk, particularly at the lateral aspect of the joint.
Interestingly, in the AI-based registration and 3D segmentation (J), this
pattern of disk displacement becomes much more evident. The disk is shown to be
laterally displaced and, in the sagittal plane, anteriorly displaced on the
lateral side, consistent with the radiologist’s suspicion but now more
clearly visualized. This case demonstrates the potential clinical value of multimodal fusion
imaging. The AI-enhanced 3D visualization allowed for a more definitive
identification of subtle displacements that were only tentatively described in
the MRI report. In complex TMJ presentations such as this, a multimodal approach
could lead to more confident and accurate diagnosis, particularly when
radiological findings are ambiguous. CASE II In case II ( Figure 8 ) it is
possible to see more slight but still significant differences, when comparing
the grey level image superimpositions (C-H) there is a very subtle tilt in the
second image that makes it so the disk fits alongside the condyle slightly
better, in the first image it is possible to see the disk overlapping with the
condyle slightly. This is confirmed when looking at the segmented models (D-J),
where in the manual approximation we can note a slight interference between the
disk model and the condyle, in the AI registration this cannot be noted, in
these models it is also possible to note a more centered disk in the AI
registration. In the 3d volumes (K-L) it is possible to see a small downward
pitch in the MRI, which makes alignment slightly off, on the AI registration (L)
we can note a very good fit around the anatomical reference points, notably the
condyle, coronoid process and external acoustic meatus. This case presents fewer notable changes than Case 1; nonetheless, those
changes are still significant enough to influence a possible diagnosis. Patient 2 presented with severe pain in the TMJ and facial region. She
had previously undergone orthognathic surgery due to a combination of maxillary
hyperplasia and mandibular hypoplasia. Despite surgery, the mandible still
appears retruded, although her occlusal relationship remains functionally
stable, albeit with a steep occlusal plane. Clinical analysis of the left TMJ revealed significant condylar
resorption and increased vertical joint space. This is clearly visible in the
imaging, particularly in (H), where the condyle appears severely resorbed,
especially in its middle-lateral portion. A prominent dark space between the
disk and the articular fossa further supports this. The clinician noted difficulty identifying the disk, which is a common
challenge when relying solely on MRI. The dark borders of the condyle and fossa
can obscure soft tissue structures like a thinned or reabsorbed disk. However,
in the fused image (H), the spatial boundaries of the joint are much clearer,
allowing for better visualization of what appears to be a posteriorly positioned
and likely reabsorbed disk. This case demonstrates how multimodal registration enhances diagnostic
certainty, particularly in joints with complex anatomical remodeling. This is a case where multimodal integration could have made diagnosis
significantly easier for the clinician. CASE III Our third case ( Figure 9 ) presents
another example of the improvement of the condyle-disk relation by the AI model
registration, in the grey level image superimpositions (C-H), it is possible to
visualize that on the manual registration (C) there is overlap of the disk with
the anterosuperior part of the condyle, It is also possible to see that the disk
seems to be pressing against the posterior and inferior part of the condylar
head. Suggesting a mixture of linear and angular misalignments, while in the AI
registration (H) it is possible to visualize a much smoother integration, where
the disk sits comfortably between the condylar head and the articular fossa. In
the 3d models (D-J) it is possible to visualize that in the Manual
superimposition (D) the disk tips laterally, seeming to almost detach from the
condyle laterally, suggesting excessive roll, and it is also possible to see the
disk pressing against the anterior portion of the condylar head, suggesting
excessive pitch. Meanwhile, on the AI registration (J) we can see a more
harmonious fit on the condylar head. On the 3d volume rendering, it is difficult
to visualize the manual registration (E-F) however, specially looking at the
frontal view there is distinguishable pitch affecting alignment, meanwhile on
the AI registration (K-L) we can see a good fit, especially in the condyle,
coronoid process and external acoustic meatus, it is also possible to visualize
muscle strands descending the temporal bone. Considering the anatomical
position, it is likely that we are visualizing a part of the temporal
muscle. In this case, we can see that although clinicians tend to focus more on
the sagittal view, yaw and roll can be just as significant in disrupting
appropriate alignment. Patient 3 reported severe headaches, TMJ pain, ear discomfort, and
occasional vertigo. Despite these symptoms, her occlusion appeared functionally
stable, with only minor anterior crowding in the lower arch. Clinically, her
soft tissue profile suggested anteroposterior skeletal deficiency of both the
maxilla and mandible, though this was not prominent on CBCT analysis. Initial TMJ assessment by the clinician noted mild sclerosis on the
superior surface of the condyle and raised suspicion of anterior disk
displacement. However, the exact position of the disk remained unclear. Upon reviewing the fused MRI-CBCT data, we observe distinct anatomical
features that clarify the diagnosis. There is marked flattening on the superior
surface of the condyle and, notably, additional flattening along the lateral
condylar walls (J). The disk does not appear to be anteriorly displaced in this
sagittal slice, but instead shows signs of medial displacement, which would have
been difficult to confirm using sagittal MRI alone. This case highlights how multimodal image fusion can uncover subtle
spatial changes, such as lateral or medial shifts, that may elude traditional
single-modality interpretation. Such findings have direct relevance for accurate
diagnosis and treatment planning in complex TMJ presentations. CASE IV Our fourth case ( Figure 10 )
presents a fascinating challenge. Here, we observe that the position of the
condyle varies slightly between the CT and the MRI. This discrepancy is likely
due to slight patient movement during the MRI acquisition, which is
understandable given that MRI scans are longer procedures and can be difficult
for some patients to remain completely still. However, even in such cases,
regional registration can still yield meaningful alignment. In the grey-level superimpositions (C and H), the manual registration
(C) shows a slight interposition between the disk and the articular fossa. In
contrast, the AI registration (H) displays a clearer and more distinct outline
between the articular fossa and the disk, indicating a superior fit. In the 3D models (D and J), the manual approximation reveals that the
disk seems to disengage from the condyle posteriorly and encroach upon the
boundaries of the articular fossa. On the other hand, the AI registration
presents a more harmonious interaction between the disk and condyle, with no
visible interposition, suggesting a more anatomically plausible
relationship. In the volume renderings (E-F and K-L), a slight upward pitch is noted
in both manual and AI registrations. This is likely an adaptive tilt to
compensate for the mismatch in condylar positioning between the two imaging
modalities. Despite this, the AI-based volume rendering shows a coherent and
continuous alignment of the zygomatic arch and condyle, reinforcing the notion
that successful regional registration was achieved despite anatomical
variability. Patient 4 presented with limited mouth opening, biweekly headaches
(described as pressure “behind the eyes”), clicking sounds in the
TMJ, arthritis in the knees, and severe jaw pain. She characterized the TMJ pain
as “a pain that makes me want to cry,” with symptoms aggravated by
chewing and yawning. In response, she adopted a soft diet and regularly used
ibuprofen and Tylenol for relief. Interestingly, she noted that as her
mouth-opening range decreased, the TMJ clicking became less frequent. A clinical MRI analysis by a TMJ specialist indicated that the condyle
was in centric relation, the disk was anteriorly displaced, and the disk
appeared macerated, although no significant effusion was observed. In our fused multimodal analysis (G–L), signs of condylar
resorption become more evident. In sagittal view, a distinct dent can be seen on
the anterior surface of the condylar head. The disk appears anteriorly
displaced, potentially adapting to fill the irregularity left by resorptive
change. Furthermore, the disk shows clear signs of thinning and surface damage,
particularly on its anterior aspect, consistent with maceration. This case illustrates how multimodal integration can not only confirm
clinical impressions but also deepen understanding of intra-articular dynamics,
providing a more nuanced view of structural degeneration in symptomatic
patients. CASE V Our final case ( Figure 11 )
underscores the importance of analyzing axial and coronal views with the same
rigor as sagittal slices. In the grey-level registrations (C and H), the manual
approximation (C) presents a less defined interface, with overlapping regions
between the condylar head and articular fossa. In contrast, the AI-based
registration (H) demonstrates a smoother and more consistent integration of the
disk, condyle, and fossa. In the 3D segmentations, attention to the coronal plane becomes
essential. In the manually approximated model (D), the disk appears to protrude
anteriorly, as if “escaping” the anatomical region, and there is a
clear tipping of the disk, suggesting roll. Meanwhile, in the AI-registered
model (J), the disk maintains a more harmonious relationship with the condyle,
without aggressive angular deviations. The 3D volume renderings (F-L) further illustrate this difference. In
the manual registration (F), the overlay fails to conform accurately to the
mandibular contour, particularly along the lateral ramus and gonial angle. In
contrast, the AI registration (L) shows a precise and stable fit across multiple
landmarks, including the condyle, coronoid process, and zygomatic arch. This case reinforces a critical principle in TMJ imaging: high-quality
registration depends on multi-planar consistency. Even minor rotational
distortions can compromise anatomical interpretation, and AI registration
methods can offer greater stability and reproducibility across dimensions when
such variability is present. Our final patient presented with severe TMJ pain and significant
functional limitations. She described extreme difficulty in eating, noting that
chewing causes intense pain. To cope, she cuts food into small pieces or relies
on liquid alternatives. She also reported pain radiating to the back of her neck
and behind her eyes, along with habitual teeth grinding. A bite splint
prescribed for sleep has been uncomfortable and disrupts her rest. Previous
interventions, including massage therapy and chiropractic treatment, were
ineffective. She has not yet attempted physical therapy. An MRI was requested by the clinician to assess her TMJ condition.
Expert interpretation noted condylar degeneration, slight anterior positioning
of the condyle, and minimal effusion. Upon review of the AI-enhanced multimodal images (G-L), several findings
stand out. There is notable condylar resorption, particularly along the
anterosuperior and lateral surfaces, giving the condylar head a more triangular
appearance. The articular disk shows clear anterior displacement, appearing to
protrude away from the condyle in the closed-mouth position, a pattern
consistent with internal derangement and likely disc-condyle incoordination. In this final case, we once again observe how 3D fusion imaging not only
confirms clinical suspicion but adds precision to the characterization of
intra-articular structures. For patients with complex, persistent pain and
inconclusive therapeutic responses, such enhanced imaging can be pivotal for
redefining treatment strategies. POTENTIAL FOR FUTURE STUDIES IN ORTHODONTICS Combined multimodal imaging analysis could greatly benefit other orthodontic
interventions, such as mandibular advancement. Very few studies have explored the
positional changes of the condyle in relation to the articular disk and surrounding
structures. Among the limited literature available, most studies have been conducted
on animal models 16 , while human
studies have typically relied on a single imaging modality 17 . The integration of CBCT and MRI now allows
for detailed, minimally invasive evaluations in human subjects. This advancement opens the door to new investigations into how the articular
disk responds when the condyle is propelled out of the fossa including how long it
takes for the disk-fossa relationship to reestablish itself after functional
advancement. Additionally, future research could explore whether different
functional appliances or advancement protocols (e.g., single-step vs. gradual
advancement) produce distinct effects on the delicate environment of the
temporomandibular joint. AI in Orthodontics Beyond multimodal registration capabilities, artificial intelligence
presents numerous opportunities for enhancing orthodontic diagnosis and
treatment planning. Within the “Automated Dental Tools” module,
several AI-powered functionalities are available, including
“AREG,” 18 which enables CBCT superimposition;
“AMASS,” 15 which facilitates automated CBCT segmentation; and
“ALI,” 19 
which provides landmark identification within CBCT scans. Additionally, the
DocShapeAXI model 19 enables
automatic classification of airway restriction due to adenoid
hypertrophy 20 , our
research team has developed an open-source, comprehensive, patient-specific
prediction model for temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis
progression. 21 
Existing AI models have also demonstrated utility in traditional cephalometric
evaluation, with recent studies indicating acceptable performance, though they
continue to require clinician validation for clinical implementation. 22 Similarly, advancements in
AI-assisted panoramic radiograph analysis have shown promise, particularly in
models designed to detect periodontal bone loss, which demonstrate effective
pathology identification despite inconsistent performance across datasets that
necessitates caution and close clinician supervision. 23 These represent significant recent
advancements in the integration of artificial intelligence and dentistry, with
future developments potentially including automated classification of skeletal
growth patterns, prediction of cases suitable for fixed
mechanotherapy, 24 and
osteoporosis screening from panoramic radiographs among other exciting and
clinically relevant innovations that promise to advance diagnostic capabilities
and treatment planning precision in orthodontic practice. Generalizability This case series illustrates the clinical applicability of our AI-based
registration model. While its quantitative performance has been previously
validated in the literature 9 ,
the effectiveness of the CBCT-MRI registration process in practice may still
depend on factors such as image quality, the clinician’s adherence to
usage guidelines, and the level of care applied during visual inspection of each
case. Rather than replacing clinical judgment, the model is designed to augment
it, providing a consistent, automated foundation upon which individualized
evaluation can be performed. Limitations Study Limitations The current study has several limitations that must be considered
during interpretation. First, the sample size remains limited, which may
affect the generalizability of our findings. The retrospective nature of
this study introduces inherent biases associated with historical data
collection. While the pipeline is largely automated, the 3D integration and
segmentation processes still require some degree of human interaction for
optimal visualization and integration of both CBCT and MRI models into a
unified view. Finally, the inherent complexity of MRI interpretation
presents a significant challenge for general dentists who may lack
specialized training in magnetic resonance imaging analysis. 25 Conclusions This pilot study presents MR2CBCT , an open-source
multimodal registration module designed to integrate MRI and CBCT images within the
3D Slicer platform. By enabling accessible, semi-automated fusion of soft and hard
tissue data, the tool addresses a critical gap in clinical imaging workflows,
especially in the context of temporomandibular joint evaluation. As demonstrated,
this platform allows clinicians to extract richer diagnostic information,
potentially improving treatment planning for complex craniofacial cases.
