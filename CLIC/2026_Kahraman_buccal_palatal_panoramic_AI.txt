Background
Third-dimensional binary localization, determining whether impacted maxillary canines (IMCs) requiring surgical exposure lie buccally or palatally, is among the key parameters influencing the choice of surgical approach. In 30–50% of cases, the tooth is non-palpable, necessitating further radiographic evaluation. This study investigated whether convolutional neural networks (CNNs) applied to panoramic radiographs (PRs) can accurately classify IMC position, enhancing their diagnostic value in preoperative assessment.

Methods
This retrospective study included 494 IMCs from 472 PRs. Surgical notes and CBCT findings served as reference standards. IMCs were annotated on PRs and classified using CNN architectures across multiple preprocessing pipelines. Deep feature embeddings were further evaluated using logistic regression (LR), support vector machines (SVM), and k-nearest neighbors (KNN). Model performance was assessed using accuracy, macro-averaged precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC.

Results
The ResNet50 + LR model yielded the highest performance (accuracy: 0.898, F1-score: 0.897, ROC-AUC: 0.945), outperforming InceptionV3 and all softmax-based models. Preprocessing had minimal effect on ResNet50 but improved InceptionV3 outcomes. Most misclassifications occurred in buccal cases. Feature-space analysis revealed favorable linear separability for LR.

Conclusions
CNN-based analysis of PRs enables accurate IMC localization, with ResNet50 + LR demonstrating consistent performance. These findings support the potential of AI-assisted PR interpretation as a viable step toward enhancing clinical utility of two-dimensional imaging.

Background
Impacted maxillary canines (IMCs), seen in 1% to 3.5% of the population [1], are critical for occlusion, aesthetics, and functional balance. Their impaction leads to equally significant problems, including occlusal disruption, cyst formation, and root resorption of adjacent incisors in up to 66% of cases [2]. Management typically requires a multidisciplinary approach involving orthodontists, surgeons, and radiologists to guide diagnosis and intervention.
When surgical exposure is required, accurate localization of the IMC guides the decision to proceed with a buccal or palatal surgical approach (SA). In 30–50% of cases, the tooth is non-palpable, necessitating further radiographic evaluation [3]. Several two-dimensional (2D) imaging methods like occlusal views and cephalometric radiographs or parallax technique assist in localization but offer limited precision [4]. Consequently, Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) is increasingly adopted for its high-resolution detail [5]; despite its greater radiation exposure, higher cost, and uncertain surgical benefit [6]. This is concerning given the predominantly young population of orthodontics, highlighting the need to minimize radiation exposure. Even low-dose CBCT protocols contribute to cumulative dose when added to the initial panoramic radiography (PR), reinforcing the need for more selective diagnostic strategies.
Recent AI advances have enhanced the diagnostic utility of single PRs, achieving accuracies of 93.67% for tooth identification, 91.5% for caries detection, 89.29% for osteoporosis assessment, and showing further potential in identifying maxillary sinusitis, periodontal bone loss, and detecting IMCs with up to 84% accuracy using convolutional neural network (CNN)-based models [7–10]. These findings prompt this question: can AI-driven PR analysis reduce reliance on CBCT for IMC surgical planning?
The rationale for this question is based on radiographic projection geometry. Teeth positioned closer to the X-ray tube and farther from the image receptor appear magnified, whereas those nearer the receptor appear smaller. Thus, an impacted canine appearing larger than its contralateral tooth likely lies palatally (tube side), while a smaller appearance suggests a buccal position [11] (Fig. 1). Morphometric analyses have shown stable proportional relationships among maxillary anterior teeth, with canines maintaining consistent ratios to adjacent incisors [12], and no significant mesiodistal variation between contralateral canines [13]. These findings support that apparent dimensional differences PRs are projection-related rather than anatomical. Such geometric cues may be detectable by AI, enhancing diagnostic precision.

There are some other radiographic indicators that may assist in bucco-palatal localization. Palatally IMCs are often positioned higher relative to the occlusal plane, and overlapping with or root resorption of the lateral incisor may further suggest a palatal trajectory (Fig. 1), both serving as indirect cues [14, 15]. These features, while subtle, may contribute to spatial interpretation when evaluated systematically [16]. Building on such anatomical and radiographic heuristics, earlier studies have attempted to localize impacted canines using conventional PR criteria, reporting accuracy rates ranging from 68% to 80% [11, 17, 18].
Based on these premises, this study investigates whether AI-based analysis of a single PR can accurately classify IMC localization as buccal or palatal. It evaluates the method’s potential to improve the clinical utility of initial PR inherent to dental examination.

Methods
Study design and setting
This retrospective study was conducted at two tertiary university hospitals in Istanbul, Türkiye: Medeniyet University and Medipol University. Records obtained between January 2015 and December 2023 were screened. Model development and reporting followed the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) guideline [19].

Image sets and eligibility criteria
Eligible image sets included paired PR and CBCT scans with at least one IMC. Inclusion criteria comprised cases with an indication for surgical exposure, as documented, along with complete clinical and surgical records and a minimum of three months of uneventful postoperative follow-up. CBCT referral forms had to cite diagnostic intent, such as IMC localization, non-palpable canine evaluation, or surgical planning. Image sets were excluded if the CBCT or PR was missing, if research consent was not documented, if anatomical or pathological interference (e.g., cleft palate or odontogenic cyst) obscured the region of interest, if image quality was degraded by motion artifacts, or with complications recorded post operative. Eligibility screening was conducted manually by an experienced dentomaxillofacial radiologist prior to annotation and modeling.
CBCT scans were obtained using: (1) i-CAT Next Generation (120 kVp, 20.27 mA, 14.7 s, FOV: 16 × 8 cm, voxel: 0.2 mm); (2) Planmeca ProMax 3D Mid (90 kVp, 10 mA, ~ 12 s, voxel: 0.4 mm). PRs were acquired via: (1) VistaPano S (72 kVp, 11 mA, 7 s); (2) Planmeca ProMax 2D (68 kVp, 14 mA, 12 s). Raw DICOMs and PR images were exported without overlaid annotations. All data were anonymized before analysis by removing identifiers from metadata, filenames, and overlays. Demographic characteristics were recorded but are not central to the modeling objective and are therefore not reported.

Reference standard, data partitioning and annotation
The reference standard was derived from retrospective surgical records documented as the SR, representing the definitive intraoperative confirmation of localization. To reflect the clinical workflow in which surgeons determine the surgical approach through CBCT-supported evaluation, an independent, blinded expert review of CBCT scans was conducted as a prognostic reliability component. This approach followed adjudication-based frameworks recommended for strengthening reference standards in radiologic validation studies [20]. The review panel included two oral and maxillofacial surgeons (S1, S2) who were not involved in the surgeries and one independent radiologist (R1), each with over ten years of clinical experience. All assessments were performed in accordance with established CBCT interpretation principles and recommended reporting guidelines [21, 22].
Inter-rater reliability among the four label sources (SR, S1, S2, and R1) was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa [23]. The computed κ was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.78 to 0.86), indicating substantial to near-perfect agreement [24]. Label distribution showed a moderate predominance of buccal cases (ratio 1.4:1), which did not meaningfully affect agreement metrics. Intra-rater reliability was assessed by having each expert (S1, S2, R1) independently re-evaluate a random subset comprising 20% of the dataset after a three-week interval. Using a two-way mixed-effects model with single measures and absolute agreement [ICC (3,1)] [25], ICC values ranged from 0.85 to 0.91, indicating consistently high temporal reliability across raters. PRs were divided into two non-overlapping groups based on the validated buccal or palatal label.
A dentomaxillofacial radiologist with over five years of experience (R2) assigned the finalized labels onto the PRs using LabelImg, a publicly available graphical image annotation tool [26]. Bounding boxes were saved in PascalVOC (XML) format and labeled as “Buc” or “Pal”. ROIs were cropped and resized for model input (Fig. 2).

Primary outcome was defined as binary classification of IMC’s surgical access (buccal vs. palatal) using PR images. Secondary outcomes included diagnostic performance metrics for each model evaluated on the test set.

Data preprocessing and model architecture
A modular CNN architecture was implemented with backbone selection (InceptionV3 [27] or ResNet50 [28]) and configurable dropout and dense layers. The pipeline began with data augmentation (horizontal flip, random height/width distortion) to enhance generalization.
All PR-based regions of interest (ROIs) were manually cropped and resized to meet the input requirements of the CNNs, following previous evidence on impacted canine detection [9]. Additional preprocessing steps included median filtering for noise suppression, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) for local contrast enhancement, and Gaussian thresholding for intensity-based segmentation. Binary masks (20% opacity) were optionally overlaid to improve texture visibility without altering anatomical fidelity, and rescaling was used to normalize intensity distributions.
Different combinations of these preprocessing steps were systematically applied to evaluate their effect on model performance.
Two training paradigms were implemented to ensure a fair and symmetric comparison between architectures. The first was an end-to-end CNN in which the pretrained backbone and dense layers were trained jointly with a SoftMax output layer (NN). The second used frozen backbone embeddings from the penultimate layer to train classical classifiers: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), and k-nearest neighbor (KNN). All models used identical preprocessing, data partitions, hyperparameter ranges, and random seeds to isolate the effect of the backbone from that of the classifier. The complete experimental design, including preprocessing, model training, and classifier integration, is summarized in Fig. 3.

Model training and evaluation
Model training was conducted using the Adam optimizer with a tunable learning rate. Sparse categorical crossentropy was used as the loss function, and accuracy served as the primary training metric. Training ran for 70 epochs with a batch size of 32 using mini-batch gradient descent. Early stopping based on validation loss (patience: 10) was applied. A fixed seed ensured reproducibility.
Each CNN was trained across 25 randomly sampled hyperparameter configurations per preprocessing pipeline. The search space included dropout rates (0.1–0.4), dense layer sizes (16–128), L2 regularization strengths (0–0.001), and learning rates (0.0001–0.01). Sensitivity analyses evaluated the impact of hyperparameters and preprocessing strategies. Final model selection was based on the highest macro-averaged F1-score on the internal validation set. No ensembling was applied; all metrics reflect individual model performance. No external dataset was used.
Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. To account for class imbalance, macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score were reported.
Deep feature embeddings were extracted from the penultimate CNN layer and saved in NumPy format. These embeddings were standardized using StandardScaler and used to train classical classifiers: support vector machines (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and logistic regression (LR). Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), retaining 95% of total variance. Classifier performance was assessed using the same evaluation metrics, with macro-averaged F1-score used for comparison. All model training and evaluation procedures were implemented in Python (v3.11.5) using TensorFlow (v2.14) and Scikit-learn (v1.2.2). LabelImg was used for manual annotation, and NumPy (v1.25.2) was used for feature storage and processing.

Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using Python (v3.11.5; SciPy, Statsmodels) and SPSS (v28.0, IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics, including the mean, standard deviation, 95% confidence intervals, and observed ranges, were reported for each preprocessing pipeline and classifier configuration to assess performance consistency.
The assumption of homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. As this assumption was violated (p < 0.001), Welch’s ANOVA was applied to compare macro-F1 scores across preprocessing pipelines, with Brown–Forsythe statistics used to confirm. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Dunnett-C test to account for unequal variances, and effect sizes were calculated using eta-squared (η²) to quantify the magnitude of group differences.

Results
A total of 472 PR-CBCT pairs yielded 494 IMC regions, including bilateral cases. All 494 regions were manually labeled, comprising 289 (58.5%) buccal and 205 (41.5%) palatal impactions. The dataset was split into training (n = 296), validation (n = 99), and test (n = 99) sets.
Statistical comparisons of eight preprocessing pipeline combinations applied to the ResNet50 model revealed significant group differences (Welch’s F (7, 335.28) = 63.38, p < 0.001, η² = 0.33). Further preprocessing did not enhance performance, and the observed differences were largely attributable to performance degradation in configurations incorporating Gaussian thresholding (Table 1).

Discussion
This study builds on prior work in IMC localization using single PR [3, 14, 15, 17, 18, 29], providing a refined and clinically relevant contribution. Among all tested configurations, the ResNet50 + LR model achieved the highest performance, with an F1-score of 0.897 and a ROC-AUC of 0.945. These values exceed standard thresholds for outstanding discrimination [30] and remained stable across repeated runs, supporting the model’s potential clinical utility. In comparison, conventional PR-based methods relying on crown magnification, angulation, or superimposition have reported lower accuracy levels, typically ranging between 68% and 80% [11, 16–18], suggesting that the proposed AI model outperforms traditional heuristic approaches. This advantage aligns with prior findings that a ResNet50-based model outperformed human experts in identifying the molar–inferior alveolar nerve relationship [31].
ResNet-based models have shown consistently strong performance in dental imaging, outperforming earlier CNN architectures due to their residual learning design, which enhances gradient flow and enables deeper, more effective feature extraction [32]. Mahdi et al. reported improved precision and F1-score when increasing model depth from ResNet50 to ResNet101 (0.978 to 0.982), with minimal computational trade-off [33]. Recent high-performing frameworks, such as Mask R-CNN with a ResNet50 backbone, have achieved F1-scores as high as 99.3% for tooth detection and numbering on PRs [34], highlighting ResNet’s effectiveness and adaptability in this domain.
Misclassifications were predominantly observed in buccal cases misidentified as palatal, mirroring the pattern reported in conventional prediction studies that relied on PR-based visual cues [11, 16–18]. Given that palatal impactions are reported to occur approximately four times more frequently than buccal ones [35], achieving high classification performance in these cases may carry greater clinical significance.
In our study, preprocessing offered no measurable benefit to ResNet50 performance, and in some configurations, particularly those involving Gaussian thresholding, it was associated with reduced classification accuracy. While light noise suppression through median filtering may slightly improve image clarity, excessive preprocessing appears to obscure subtle features essential for model learning. In our radiographic dataset, where fine grayscale variations are diagnostically meaningful, this effect was especially evident. Gaussian thresholding, by eliminating intermediate gray levels, likely distorted key structural and intensity gradients, ultimately impairing feature extraction. Preserving grayscale richness therefore remains critical for achieving optimal performance in radiographic models. These results are consistent with previous studies showing that ResNet50 can extract effective features from nearly raw data with little need for preprocessing. Earlier work reported only marginal accuracy gains with Gaussian filtering (78.74–80.02%) and even performance decline with median filtering (74.89%) [36]. Similarly, in glaucoma detection, ResNet50 maintained stable outcomes across different preprocessing settings [37]. In contrast, Inception-based architectures such as InceptionV3 and GoogleNet tended to benefit from input enhancement and achieved slightly better generalization in external datasets [36, 38]. Altogether, these findings indicate that the effect of preprocessing depends on the model architecture: while Inception-based networks appear more responsive to contrast and noise adjustments, ResNet50 performs best when trained on minimally processed data.
The relatively strong performance of LR on ResNet50-derived embeddings may reflect a feature space that is approximately linearly separable, enabling simple classifiers to perform competitively. Prior work suggests that deep CNNs, particularly when trained with contrastive or transfer learning, can generate embeddings where semantically similar instances form compact and discriminative clusters [39]. This observation is further supported by recent applications in medical image analysis, where ResNet50 features combined with LR achieved high accuracy in classifying transplantable versus non-transplantable liver grafts [40].
Several limitations merit consideration. While our dataset originates from two institutions, enhancing clinical and technical heterogeneity, its overall size remains modest by deep learning standards and lacks validation on an independent external cohort. Prior studies in radiology have shown that algorithmic performance frequently diminishes on external data, with 81% of models exhibiting reduced accuracy and a median drop of 11.4% [41]. As such, future work should include prospective, multi-institutional validation to rigorously assess generalizability and clinical applicability. Building on this, it is important to recognize that clinical decisions are inherently multifactorial and often rely on a combination of anatomical assessment, surgical experience, and contextual judgment. Although the model captured the binary distinction between buccal and palatal impactions, surgical access decisions are rarely determined by this parameter alone. Moreover, as preoperative CBCT contributes to both surgical planning and retrospective evaluation in our design, a degree of partial dependency between datasets cannot be entirely excluded. External validation using independent cohorts will be essential to examine whether the learned features generalize across diverse clinical contexts and decision-making patterns. Ultimately, while complete automation of such decisions is unlikely due to their context-dependent nature, the present model provides a foundational step toward integrating objective, imaging-based insights into surgical planning. Additionally, subtle anatomical variations among cases cannot be entirely excluded and may have introduced minor variability into the model’s predictions.
A second limitation lies in the model’s lack of interpretability. While classification performance was the primary aim, no analysis was conducted to elucidate the network’s decision-making process. The opaque nature of deep learning remains a barrier to clinical integration. Incorporating explainable AI (XAI) techniques, such as saliency mapping or SHAP analysis, could improve transparency by revealing anatomically relevant features that drive predictions. In medical imaging, XAI has been instrumental in exposing model weaknesses, refining training strategies, and aligning outputs with clinically meaningful structures. Future work should adopt such frameworks to ensure model focus corresponds to diagnostic landmarks, such as the crown and root of the IMC. Advances may also stem from architectures that better capture spatial context and anatomical relationships, including attention mechanisms, coordinate convolutions, and transformer-based modules. Integrating clinician-informed variables, such as tooth angulation or proximity to adjacent roots, may further enhance clinical relevance through hybrid modeling [42].

Conclusions
The proposed CNN-based approach demonstrated stable internal performance in the binary localization (buccal or palatal) of IMCs on PRs. While these results support its potential to enhance the clinical utility of panoramic radiographs in routine assessment, external multi-center validation and prospective evaluation are needed to confirm its broader applicability.
