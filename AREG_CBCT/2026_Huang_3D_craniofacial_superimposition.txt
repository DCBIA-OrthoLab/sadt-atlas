Automated AI-driven 3D craniofacial superimposition for clinically useful treatment outcomes in growing patients: A multicenter study

ABSTRACT
Introduction: In growing patients, reliable quantification of change requires explicitly stating the reference used for superimposition and interpreting all values as relative changes; however, manual workflows are time-consuming and variable. This study assessed measurement reliability and workflow efficiency for reference-explicit analyses, comparing a fully automated, open-source segmentation and registration workflow against a semi-automatic voxel-based approach for clinically useful three-dimensional assessments. Methods: Twenty-two Class II patients with cone-beam computed tomographies at the pretreatment (T1) and posttreatment (T2) were analyzed. Automated segmentation and voxel-based superimposition were performed with built-in quantitative analysis. Primary outcomes were skeletal and dental changes relative to cranial base and regional maxilla and mandibular superimposition. Three registration approaches incorporating varying levels of AI involvement - conventional, semi-automated, and fully automated – were compared. Performances were assessed by mixed-effects linear regression models. Results: Agreement for skeletal and dental measurements was high, with minor differences observed between AI-driven and conventional registration approaches and all methods showing clinically comparable precision. Most absolute average differences between automated and conventional workflows under 1.5 mm for linear and 1.5° for angular measurements. Cranial base superimposition differences showed an average difference in T2-T1 changes ranging from - 0.3 to 0.7 mm, while regional superimposition differences showed an average difference in T2-T1 changes ranging from −1.7 to 1.1 mm. Conclusions: Automated clinician-verified workflow yields reliable and faster 3D changes measures in growing patients. Interpretation must consider the reference region used for superimposition. AI-driven open-source tools provides a practical quantitative analysis to support diagnosis, timing, and assessment of treatment outcomes.

## Introduction
 Three-dimensional (3D) superimposition enables longitudinal documentation of craniofacial change in growing patients. In clinical orthodontics, practitioners routinely use cranial base and regional maxillary/mandibular superimpositions to visualize change between time points and to communicate progress with patients and teams. 1 , 2 Prior work in growing patient cohorts has relied on cranial base, maxillary, and mandibular superimpositions to quantify displacement patterns, which underscores the expectation that the chosen reference be stated explicitly. 3 – 7 

 Traditional two-dimensional cephalometric superimpositions are inherently subjective and qualitative because of projection and measurement constraints: landmark overlap and ambiguity, magnification and parallax, head-posture variability, and the inability to represent transverse changes or true 3D rotations. 8 Even when standardized, these factors introduce observer dependence and hinder reproducibility. In growing patients, an additional challenge is that the anatomic surfaces used for alignment remodel over time; therefore, all measurements must be interpreted as relative displacement with respect to the chosen reference. 1 , 9 , 10 

 Earlier 3D studies demonstrated the feasibility and value of voxel-based and surface-based superimpositions for quantifying craniofacial change. 11 However, those methods often required multiple software steps, semi-manual segmentation/registration adjustments, and substantial diagnostic time, limiting routine clinical friendliness and scalability The present work builds on that foundation by testing an open-source, automated segmentation and superimposition workflow with a standardized clinician quantitative analysis to reduce time-on-task and operator dependence, reporting the changes relative to reference regions.

 Clinics need a standardized, open-source workflow that (i) makes the reference system (cranial base vs. regional) balance 12 – 14 explicit, (ii) produces reliable relative-displacement 15 – 20 measurements with a built-in clinician verification step to confirm registration accuracy, and (iii) can be completed in a realistic amount of review time. Importantly, such a workflow is intended for measurement standardization and documentation; it is not designed to separate growth from treatment effects or to evaluate the effectiveness of specific protocols. This study assesses whether a fully automated, open-source segmentation + registration 21 – 22 workflow, followed by a mandatory clinician verification step for registration accuracy, can deliver agreement-comparable and efficient reference-explicit 3D displacement measurements in growing Class II patients when compared with a semiautomatic voxel-based approach. These innovations will not only streamline workflows but also allow researchers and clinicians to conduct more rigorous morphological comparisons and make more accurate clinical diagnoses based on unbiased assessments of treatment outcomes. Before these innovations can be implemented in clinical practice, it is essential to evaluate their performance, which is the primary aim of this study.

## Material and Methods
 This retrospective study was designed to assess the performance of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) registration approaches incorporating varying levels of AI 21 , 23 . Three different registration approaches used are shown in Figure 1 . This multicenter study performs external validation of previously published segmentation and registration methodologies ( Figure 1 ) using 44 CBCT from 22 patients with Class II malocclusion at the pretreatment and posttreatment to correct maxillomandibular discrepancies at the University of Minnesota. 24 Institutional review board approval was granted by the University of Michigan (HUM00146139). Patients were included if Class II molar relationship with ANB ≥ 4.75°, SN-GoGn &lt; 37°, a cervical vertebral maturation stage of 2–4, and received comprehensive orthodontic treatment with a cervical pull facebow headgear appliance. 24 Exclusion criteria 24 included crossbite, history of facial trauma or medical conditions that affected growth, orthodontic/orthopedic treatment prior to the baseline, or cases with the needs of surgical/extraction treatment. Patient demographics 24 are shown in Table I . For each patient, de-identified full-face large field-of-view (FOV) CBCT images were acquired at the pretreatment (T1) and posttreatment (T2), spanning the pubertal growth spurt.

## Superimposition reference regions.
 Because craniofacial structures remodel during growth, all reported changes are expressed as relative displacement with respect to a defined reference. 3D superimposition was performed relative to three regions of reference: cranial base 25 , mandible 26 , 27 and maxilla 8 , 28 to assess overall facial growth and treatment displacements and bone remodeling relative to the cranial base and regional changes relative to the maxilla and the mandible. 12 – 14 For cranial base superimposition, the anterior cranial base was selected as the reference region, given the study population of growing patients and the relative stability of this area during growth. As described by Cevidanes et al 25 , this region includes the following structures: the anterior wall of the sella, anterior clinoid processes, planum sphenoidale, lesser wings of the sphenoid, superior aspect of the ethmoid and cribriform plate, cortical ridges on the medial and superior surfaces of the orbital roofs, and the inner cortical layer of the frontal bones. For maxilla regional superimposition, the reference region was defined according to Ruellas et al 34 . This region includes the maxillary bone bounded inferiorly by the dentoalveolar processes, superiorly by the plane connecting the right and left orbitale points, laterally by the zygomatic process at the orbitale point, and posteriorly by the plane aligned with the distal surface of the second molars. For mandible regional superimposition, the mandible body mask was used as the reference region, as described by Ruellas et al 26 . The region’s boundaries are as following: inferiorly along the lower border of the mandible, superiorly below the alveolar bone processes, anterior at the mandibular symphysis, posteriorly up to but excluding the mandibular rami and condyles. This region excludes the teeth, alveolar bone, rami and condyles, thereby focusing on the basal bone of the mandible along with the stable structures such as the symphysis and inner cortical layers, while excluding areas prone to remodeling or growth changes such as the ascending rami, condylar heads, and alveolar regions supporting the teeth.

## Image analysis workflow.
 The image analysis workflow is summarized in Figure 2 . For approach 1 (conventional registration approach), the image analysis procedure was described in Bates et al 24 . This approach involves segmentations by user interactive ITK-SNAP (version 4.0.1) 22 and voxel-based SlicerCMF registration tool ( cmf.slicer.org ) 28 via 3D Slicer (version 5.2.2). This registration approach has been used in varied studies 28 , 29 . For approach 2, the semiautomatic approach was based on ITK-SNAP segmentation of the regions of reference and whole sample automated registration approach using 3D Slicer extension ‘Areg’ 21 ( https://github.com/lucanchling/AREG ). For approach 3, the fully automatic approach was based on opensource fully automated whole sample multi-anatomical skull structure segmentation and registration via 3D Slicer extensions ‘AMASSS’ 23 ( https://github.com/lucanchling/AMASSS_CBCT ) and ‘AReg’ 21 , respectively.

 Dental and skeletal landmarks ( Table II ) were identified by a single calibrated examiner on the scans registered relative to the anterior cranial base, the maxillary regional reference, and the mandibular regional reference. To minimize variability from repeated landmark placement, the transformation matrices obtained in Approaches 2 and 3 were applied to propagate the same landmark set across all registrations. In other words, for a given patient and time point, a landmark’s coordinates were fixed; only the registration transform changed. This ensured that any differences in linear or angular measurements between approaches were attributable to differences in registration, not differences in landmark identification. Linear measurements included right–left, anteroposterior, superior–inferior, and 3D Euclidean distances. Angular measurements included yaw, pitch, and roll. All measurements were computed using the AQ3DC extension for 3D Slicer ( https://github.com/DCBIA-OrthoLab/Q3DCExtension ). 30 , 31 

## Statistical Analysis
 Descriptive statistics were reported in mean and standard deviation. To compare the differences in measurements with the registration approaches, mixed-effects linear regression models were used. The outcomes were the linear/angular measurements for each patient. For each measurement, the model assumes random effects for patient and approaches and included factors that time points (pretreatment [T1] vs. posttreatment [T2]) and approaches (conventional, semiautomatic, and fully automatic registrations). This model allowed for correlation of measurements within patients and among same measurements from different approaches. For the three approaches, differences were computed between (1) semiautomatic vs. conventional, (2) fully automatic vs. conventional, and (3) fully automatic vs. semiautomatic. For each measurement, the 95% confidence intervals for the differences in measurements are reported with confidence interval excluding zero representing statistically significant difference. P values are reported with values less than 0.05 as statically significant. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2, 2024-10-31).

## Results
 Graphic displays between different registration approaches for cranial base, regional maxillary and mandibular superimpositions are shown for three cases in Figures 3 – 5 . The T2-T1 changes were illustrated by the semitransparent overlay between T2 and T1 surface models. Comparing the T2-T1 changes, Case 1 showed notable horizontal changes, Case 3 exhibited relatively greater vertical changes, while Case 2 displayed both some of horizontal and vertical changes. For all three types of growth/treatment changes, the different registrations approaches showed similar visual T2-T1 change patterns.

 For cranial base superimposition ( Table III ), all the three approaches suggested similar T2-T1 changes – clockwise rotation changes for both mandibular plane (average T2-T1 changes for three approaches: 0.8°, 0.8° and 0.5°) and palatal plane (1.9°, 1.8° and 1.6°), posterior-inferior change for A point, and anterior-inferior change for both B point and Pog. Conventional and semiautomatic registration approaches showed similar angular and linear measurements. For the difference-in-difference estimates, relative to conventional or semiautomatic registration approaches, the T2-T1 changes estimated using fully automatic registration approach had a greater difference on sagittal dimension (anteroposterior) compared with vertical dimension (superoinferior). Using A point as an example, the average T2-T1 changes on vertical dimension were similar across three approaches, while the changes on sagittal dimension were −1.3 mm, −1.2 mm, and −0.7 mm for conventional, semi- and fully automatic registration approached, respectively. A similar pattern was observed for B point and Pog, with T2-T1 changes showing more anterior change using fully automatic registration approach. However, the absolute differences in T2-T1 changes by different approaches are minor, with average linear differences ranging from 0.0 to 0.7 mm.

 For maxillary regional superimposition ( Table IV ), the average T2-T1 angular measurement changes by all three approaches showed buccal tipping of maxillary incisor (range of T2-T1 change: 8.4° - 9.8°) and mesial tipping of maxillary molars (range: 2.6° - 4.7°). The average T2-T1 linear measurement changes indicated similar pattern - antero-superior changes (buccal translation and intrusion) of maxillary incisor and anterior-inferior change (mesial translation and extrusion) of maxillary molars in general. Among the three approaches, the difference-in-difference estimates showed semi- and fully automatic registration approaches were similar in terms of the annular and linear measurements. Compared to conventional registration approach, the average T2-T1 difference for angular measurements from semi- and fully automatic registration approaches indicated the maxillary incisors/molars were less buccally/mesial tipped, with average angular differences ranging from 1.2° to 1.3°. The average T2-T1 difference for linear measurements revealed that, compared to conventional registration approach, semi- and fully automatic registration approaches resulted in less buccal positioning and greater intrusion of the maxillary incisors, as well as less mesial positioning and less extrusion of the maxillary molars. The average linear differences ranging from 0.4 to 1.4 mm. Besides, there were no differences for transverse changes across the three different approaches.

 For mandibular regional superimposition ( Table V ), the average T2-T1 angular measurement changes by all three approaches showed buccal tipping of mandibular incisor (range of T2-T1 change: 3.8° - 4.9°) and distal tipping of mandible molars (range: 2.9° - 6.0°). The average T2-T1 linear measurement changes indicated similar pattern – inferior/intrusion changes (conventional registration approach revealed an average 0.1 mm buccal change while semi-/fully automatic registration approaches showed an average 0.2/0.4 mm lingual change) of mandibular incisor and anterior-superior change (mesial change and extrusion) of mandibular molars in general. The T2-T1 changes for Condylion showed a posterior-superior change. Similar to the findings from maxillary regional superimpositions, a comparison of the three approaches showed that semi-/fully automatic registration approaches yielded comparable results. Compared to conventional registration approach, the T2-T1 difference for angular measurements from semi-/fully automatic registration approaches indicated less buccally positioning of mandibular incisors and more distally positioning of mandibular molars. The difference-in-difference estimates in average angular differences range from 0.7° to 1.2°. The difference-in-difference estimates in average linear differences showed that, compared to conventional registration approach, semi-/fully automatic registration approaches suggested the mandibular incisors were positioned more lingually and intruded, while the mandibular molars were less mesially positioned and less extruded. The average difference-in-difference estimates in linear differences range for those mandibular incisors and molars from 0.1 to 0.7 mm. As for Condylion, it was also positioned more posteriorly and less superiorly comparing semi-/fully automatic approaches to conventional one.

## Discussion
 This study evaluated the precision of an open-source AI-driven CBCT superimposition tool compared to conventional voxel-based and semiautomatic registration methods in growing Class II patients treated with cervical pull headgear. The results support the clinical applicability and reliability of automated registration techniques for efficient and precise 3D assessment of craniofacial changes, while commercial software such as Dolphin Imaging provide proprietary registration tools 32 . While all approaches utilized the open-source 3D Slicer platform and ITK-SNAP, they incorporated distinct modules and algorithms, leading to meaningful comparisons of automation levels. The conventional approach 1, as described by Bates et al. 24 , involved manual segmentation using ITK-SNAP followed by voxel-based registration via the SlicerCMF tool. The semiautomated approach 2 used ITK-SNAP segmentation but automated the registration step using the AReg extension for the whole-sample process. The fully automated approach 3 integrated AI-based segmentation via AMASSS (using UNETR models) with AReg registration, enabling batch processing without manual intervention. These difference highlight the progression toward reduced user dependency. Our study is the first investigation of a free AI approach, addressing a critical gap in accessible, cost-effective solutions for 3D orthodontic analysis.

 Overall, the whole sample semi-/fully automatic approach provides equivalent quality of image registration, reproducing previous results as reported in Bates’ paper. Comparing the three different approaches in cranial base, maxillary, and mandibular regional superimpositions, the results reveal that while the absolute differences in the T2-T2 changes exist and some of them were statistically significant, they were clinically minimal ( Figure 3 – 5 ). The present study results indicate that AI-driven workflows can achieve precision comparable to conventional voxel-based techniques while reducing time and operator dependency, a finding consistent with advancements in AI automation 33 .

 The minor discrepancies observed between the automated and conventional methods in the present study were primarily attributed to variability in segmentation and differences in registration. In particular, for regional maxillary and mandibular superimpositions, the main source of variations was associated with the segmentations process, as the differences between semiautomatic and conventional approaches were larger than those between fully and semiautomatic approaches. Additionally, larger by-approach differences were observed for landmarks farther away from the regions of reference for registrations. These results are consistent with the findings of Nguyen et al. 27 , which may be explained by the fact that small differences in segmentation of the region of reference could lead to larger discrepancies in regions located farther from the region of reference.

 Compared to individual clinician-dependent segmentation and registration, the AI-driven algorithms in this study were trained using CBCT images segmented and registered by multiple clinicians. This approach helps reduce inconsistencies in defining the boundaries of the region of reference, which could result from variations in training, fatigue or human errors 34 . Ruellas et al. 34 found that the interpretation of facial changes may depend on the chosen region of reference, especially in growing patients. Automated approaches are less biased and provide more consistent analyses, supporting their clinical application for growth assessment and treatment planning. In addition, automatic landmark identification could also significantly reduce the errors and bias in image registration. To minimized errors from landmark identification in our study, a single experienced observer identified dental and skeletal landmarks ( Table I ) on scans registered via Approach 1. Transformation matrices from Approaches 2 and 3 were then applied to these landmarks, ensuring consistent coordinates across methods and attributing measurement differences solely to registration variations rather than landmarking errors.

 The development of open-source automated segmentation (AMASSS) and registration (AReg) algorithms, as described by Gillot et al. 23 and Anchling et al. 21 , respectively, facilitate broader clinical adoption of advanced 3D analyses, particularly for practices without proprietary software licenses. However, rigorous validation is necessary to ensure interoperability across CBCT systems and diverse patient cohorts. By reducing the time and effort required for these analyses, AI-driven tools can facilitate the integration of advanced imaging techniques into clinical diagnostic and treatment planning protocols, ultimately enhancing the standard of care.

 A comprehensive understanding of craniofacial growth and treatment effects requires the assessment of complex interactions among simultaneously growing structures with differential timing 35 . While the present study primarily relied on landmark-based measurements, future investigations should incorporate more holistic 3D analyses to fully capture the spatial relationships between anatomical components and their morphogenesis in response to treatment. Key areas for future directions include incorporating real-time TMJ kinematics or soft tissue dynamics 36 , to enhance functional outcome assessments; collaborative efforts to standardize AI training datasets across institutions to mitigate bias, improve model robustness and ethical AI integration. Open-source tools must prioritize patient privacy, data anonymization, and algorithmic transparency to earn clinical trust. 37 Combining AI automation with clinician oversight, that means, AI-generated registrations reviewed by practitioners, is needed to balance efficiency with clinical expertise.

## Conclusions
 This study demonstrates the comparable accuracy of AI-driven open-source tools to conventional methods for 3D superimposition in the context of evaluating growth and treatment changes in Class II patients treated with headgear. These findings support the clinical applicability and promise of automated registration techniques for efficient and reliable 3D assessment in orthodontics. Ongoing research and validation will further elucidate the role of AI in advancing personalized diagnosis, treatment planning, and outcome evaluation, ultimately improving patient care in the era of digital orthodontics.
