## Abstract

### Objectives
The rapid evolution of large language models (LLMs) and their growing application in clinical text processing have created an urgent need for reliable de-identification mechanisms. While LLMs show promise in identifying sensitive health information (SHI), their capabilities require rigorous evaluation. This study aims to conduct a comprehensive benchmarking analysis of various LLM-based, traditional rule-based and hybrid de-identification methods.


### Methods
Our benchmark analysis used five datasets (i2b2-2006, MIMIC-2008, i2b2-2014, i2b2-2016 and OpenDeID v1) from different countries. We developed three baseline and eight LLM-based models. The experimental setup encompassed nine different settings using various combinations of training and testing sets to assess model robustness and cross-dataset performance.


### Results
In the baseline models, the approach trained on the combined corpus of all five datasets (setting 3) significantly outperformed the other settings, achieving a strict F1 micro-average score of 0.8172. Regarding LLM-based models, the supervised fine-tuning approach using the same combined configuration (setting 9) achieved the highest performance with a strict F1 score of 0.9447.


### Discussion
The harmonisation of corpora ensured standardised data formatting and SHI management across five diverse datasets, highlighting the necessity for uniform categorisation to enhance the reliability of de-identification results.


### Conclusions
Our findings indicate that while fine-tuned LLMs offer superior accuracy, the observed performance variability across heterogeneous electronic health record sources poses significant technical challenges. Real-world implementation must address these inconsistencies to overcome the ethical and technical hurdles associated with deploying LLMs for handling sensitive health data.


## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC
  - Large language models (LLMs) have shown promise for de-identifying sensitive health information (SHI) in electronic health records (EHRs), but existing evidence is limited and inconsistent across datasets and settings. Traditional rule-based and hybrid approaches are widely used, yet their performance varies, highlighting the need for systematic and comparative evaluation.


## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS
  - This study provides a comprehensive benchmark comparing LLM-based, rule-based and hybrid de-identification methods across five heterogeneous datasets using multiple training and testing configurations. It demonstrates that supervised fine-tuned LLMs trained on harmonised, combined corpora achieve substantially higher accuracy than baseline approaches, while also revealing significant performance variability across EHR sources.


## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY
  - Our findings highlight the importance of corpus harmonisation, robust evaluation and governance strategies when deploying LLMs for SHI de-identification in real-world clinical systems. Future research, implementation efforts and policy decisions must address data heterogeneity, bias and technical variability to ensure safe, ethical and reliable use of LLMs in clinical workflows.


## Introduction
Central to artificial intelligence’s (AI’s) advancements are large language models (LLMs). LLMs exhibit versatility and capability to perform a wide variety of clinical natural language processing tasks. These versatile capabilities of LLMs have transformative potential in medical education, research, clinical practice and healthcare delivery. LLMs are increasingly used to generate discharge summaries, diagnose complex clinical cases and provide teaching and training aids for medical licensing examinations. A recent review has pointed out the importance of the growing application of generative AI and LLMs in the healthcare education and practice areas to address the critical and challenging questions of trustworthiness, accountability and protection of privacy in the healthcare and medical fields. For example, the 2025 SREDH/AI Cup competition has highlighted the feasibility and persistent difficulty of accurately detecting sensitive health information (SHI) in speech data. Integrating advanced AI models with clinical information and other modalities can potentially improve diagnostic accuracy, prognostic performance and clinical decision-making compared with unimodal approaches. However, it also introduces additional challenges, especially in terms of privacy and interpretability.

Safeguarding patient privacy during data exchange and transmission is critical. In compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), our recent study highlighted that the secondary use of electronic health records (EHRs) with LLMs leads to substantial privacy risks, which necessitates robust privacy-preserving strategies such as de-identification, surrogate generation, differential privacy, federated learning and locally deployed LLMs to ensure compliance with regulatory frameworks including HIPAA and GDPR. The removal of 18 categories of SHI text notes is mandatory. LLM-based methods offer numerous advantages, such as privacy preservation. These models can accurately discern sensitive data and gain insights from extensive datasets to recognise patterns and correlations within textual components. This attribute is invaluable in de-identification endeavours, where the primary objective is to obfuscate identifying details while retaining the text’s original meaning. Moreover, LLMs exhibit swift data-processing capabilities, facilitating time efficiency and diminishing error risks for researchers and healthcare organisations. Furthermore, models can be trained on diverse datasets, enabling their utilisation across various domains. For instance, training LLMs on healthcare data renders them applicable in insurance and other sectors, enhancing their versatility and utility. In addition to prominent LLMs such as ChatGPT and GPT-4, numerous individuals employ a variety of de-identification techniques, including rule-based, machine learning (ML)-based and hybrid approaches, to effectively perform de-identification tasks. These methods encompass a spectrum of algorithms and methodologies tailored to anonymise sensitive information in textual data, catering to diverse requirements and preferences across different applications and industries.

Benchmarking ensures that the selected LLM meets the stringent accuracy and reliability standards necessary for maintaining patient confidentiality and complying with regulatory requirements, such as HIPAA. Although similar studies have been conducted using small datasets, significant variability in EHR documentation styles across different healthcare systems, ranging from structured templates to idiosyncratic clinical narratives, poses a substantial challenge for model generalisation. Furthermore, international differences in SHI category definitions (eg, US-centric HIPAA vs other regional privacy standards) reinforce the need for robust, multi-country benchmarking. To the best of our knowledge, no large-scale studies have evaluated various LLMs across multiple international datasets. We aimed to evaluate the performance of LLM-based approaches with traditional rule-based and hybrid de-identification methods across a range of datasets and analyse the scalability and computational efficiency of LLMs compared with other de-identification techniques. By systematically benchmarking LLMs against conventional de-identification methods, our research aims to provide valuable insights into the efficacy and scalability of these advanced language models.


## Methods

### Corpora
We identified five datasets comprising EHR text notes sourced from multiple healthcare institutions (). To ensure broad healthcare representation, publicly available datasets were standardised into the OpenDeID v1 corpus format. The selection process aimed to encompass varying patient demographics, medical specialties and geographical origins to ensure a broad spectrum of healthcare contexts. The datasets used in this experiment include (a) i2b2 2006 Corpus: comprising 889 deidentified discharge summaries provided as part of the 2006 i2b2 Challenge. This dataset includes annotated challenge segments, training and test sets, along with ground truth annotations. (b) MIMIC 2008 Corpus: consisting of 2434 nursing notes extracted from patients admitted to intensive care units at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Centre, Boston, Massachusetts, USA. (c) i2b2 2014 Corpus: encompassing 1304 medical records representing 296 patients, shared as part of the 2014 i2b2 Challenge. (d) 2016 CEGS N-GRID Corpus: comprising 1000 psychiatric intake records, featuring a blend of standardised questionnaires and narrative accounts of patient responses, along with clinician observations. (e) OpenDeID v1 Corpus: 2100 pathology reports derived from 1833 cancer patients across four urban Australian hospitals.


### De-identification methods
To execute the de-identification of SHI, two distinct methodologies were employed: baseline and LLM-based models. Our baselines were rule-based or traditional ML-based techniques that systematically identified and masked SHIs within the text. These methods rely on predefined rules or a hybrid of rule-based heuristics and ML algorithms to detect and obfuscate sensitive information accurately. The following baseline models were used: OpenDeID pipeline, conditional random field (CRF) model and CRF with long short-term memory network. LLM-based models use deep learning architectures to effectively analyse and mask sensitive information within textual data. The following LLMs were used for the downstream de-identification task using two approaches: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and in-context learning (ICL); Llama-2-7b, Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Phi-3-Mini-4K-Instruct, Yi-1.5, Qwen and Pythia.


#### Supervised fine-tuning
First, we adapted the pretrained LLMs to the de-identification task using the compiled training set via the SFT. To enhance the SHI recognition capability of the LLMs, we followed an instruction-based tuning approach as proposed by Lee et al. It also helps obtain a desirable output with the required SHI categories. #### In-context learning
ICL refers to the ability of LLMs to learn from a few examples in context. The key idea of the ICL is to learn from an analogy. Like the SFT template, a pretrained LLM generates the most likely response based on the given instruction and input text. The key difference is that the generation is conditioned on a demonstration set defined by the Demonstration section, as illustrated in . The template began with a clear set of instructions to define the SHI recognition task. This is followed by a series of demonstrations that are used by the model as a reference to understand the task requirements and how to execute them correctly. Finally, the model is presented with the exact input for which the LLM processes and generates output. In our implementation, when we obtain a sentence that requires de-identification, we retrieve three sentence-label pairs from the training dataset as demonstrations. For the selection of demonstrations, we selected the nearest neighbours of the input based on cosine similarity estimation based on their corresponding vector representations. For our ICL experiments, a three-shot demonstration approach was selected. Based on preliminary empirical evaluations, this configuration provided an optimal balance between model performance and computational efficiency, as increasing the number of shots beyond three resulted in marginal gains in accuracy while significantly increasing token usage and inference time.


### Experimental setup and performance evaluation
Nine experimental settings ( and ) were designed to evaluate the performance of the baseline methods and LLMs for de-identification tasks using different training and testing datasets. These settings aim to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of baselines and LLMs, with and without fine-tuning, on diverse training and testing datasets to determine the best approaches for de-identification tasks. To evaluate the effectiveness of our method, we employed a comprehensive evaluation script adapted from i2b2 evaluation scripts. This script, executed via the command line, generates micro-averaged precision, recall and F1-scores under both strict and relaxed matching criteria. Strict matching demands exact offset alignment with gold-standard annotations, whereas relaxed matching allows for character offset tolerance, accommodating minor variations. ## Results

### Observed trends in SHI categorisation and granularity
The distribution of SHI entities across categories is comprehensively documented in , which provides insights into the prevalence and distribution of sensitive information within the corpora. Lexical diversity was consistent across all datasets, as reflected by stable type-token ratio values (). The harmonisation process indicated evolving trends in SHI categorisation, particularly in the granularity of personal identifiers. We identified core medical record identifiers (MEDICALRECORD, ID, IDNUM and BIOID) common among the corpora, with additional fields (HEALTHPLAN, DEVICE, LICENSE and USERNAME) in the i2b2 2014 and i2b2 2016 datasets, to meet the needs of modern healthcare documentation. The MIMIC 2008 corpus introduced granular name subcategories—patient name (PTName), hospital staff name (HCPName) and patient’s relatives name (RelativeProxyName), showing specifications for handling personal information. Location categories also expanded to include STREET, CITY, STATE, ZIP, HOSPITAL, ORGANISATION and DEPARTMENT, while contact information evolved towards digital era identifiers (URL, EMAIL and FAX). For detailed SHI category mapping across different datasets, refer to .

Significant variations were found between corpora in the quantitative comparison of SHI distributions (). In the ID category, we found large fluctuations, with MEDICALRECORD instances ranging from 6 (i2b2-2016) to 2090 (OpenDeID_surr_v2.3). Healthcare provider information, particularly DOCTOR names, consistently maintained high frequencies across the datasets (3751–6364 instances). Location information peaked in granularity over time, with HOSPITAL being the most common location type (2312 instances in i2b2-2014 and 3523 in i2b2-2016). AGE information showed fluctuations with 5991 instances in i2b2-2016, while temporal information such as DATE had 12 482 instances in i2b2-2014. Professional information showed notable growth from 413 instances in i2b2-2014 to 2481 in i2b2-2016. Our results also revealed key trends in the evolution of the CONTACT information. The instances of traditional PHONE entries decreased from 524 in the i2b2-2014 dataset to 256 in the i2b2-2016 dataset, while instances of modern digital identifiers increased from five to seven for EMAIL. These findings suggest a shift in healthcare documentation practices with an increasing emphasis on electronic communication. Moreover, the emergence and specialisation of the ROOM and DEPARTMENT categories in other datasets suggest a trend towards finer location tracking within healthcare facilities.

### Error analysis
We identified the cause of the elevated count of false negatives (FN) by systematically analysing the errors observed in the predictions of the best-performing run (). To facilitate this analysis, we randomly selected seven reports from various configurations. It is pertinent to note that 192 FN instances were identified across seven reports. Our analysis identified nine distinct factors that contribute to the occurrence of FN. illustrates the distribution of these factors, revealing a predominant trend wherein a significant proportion of FNs stem from the tagging of SHI tokens as ‘O’ during the labelling phase of prediction. This issue accounted for approximately 62.5% of the total errors with 120 instances. There are 31 instances of incorrect tag type predictions and 11 cases where ‘B’ tags do not have corresponding end tags; it points to notable challenges in sequence labelling and boundary detection.

The error analysis of the best-performing model showed some interesting patterns. Even though setting 3 did better than setting 1 and setting 2, there were still some mistakes. For instance, it struggled with recognising IDs and often confused terms like ‘father’ or ‘partner/girlfriend’ as IDs. On the bright side, name recognition improved, with Setting 3 successfully identifying complex names like ‘Haley-Shauna Jose’, but it still mixed up non-names with names. Locations were also misidentified; for example, ‘Westinghouse Evening Programme’ got tagged as a location by mistake. Age and date recognition had its ups and downs, especially with casual formats, and profession recognition often misclassified unrelated terms as job titles. These patterns highlight the ongoing challenges we face in improving sequence labelling and accurately detecting entity boundaries across different models. As summarised in , the most frequently missed SHI categories across all configurations were ‘dates’ and ‘hospital/provider names’ due to their high structural variability in clinical narratives. These dataset-specific differences, particularly in more diverse corpora like i2b2-2014, underscore a significant practical challenge: even partial failures in identifying such categories can increase the risk of re-identification through linkage attacks. Consequently, these results emphasise that while LLM-based approaches offer clear advantages, real-world deployment still necessitates secondary validation to ensure the integrity of patient privacy.


## Discussion
Our study is unique in that it provides comprehensive benchmarking of several LLMs across a range of datasets, including i2b2 2006, MIMIC 2008, i2b2 2014, 2016 CEGS N-GRID and OpenDeID v1. Although the study aimed to benchmark several LLMs, few LLMs were excluded owing to the terms of usage imposed by them.

Dai et al and Mir et al used the OpenDeID v2 corpus, which included more reports and additional time annotations, for the SREDH/AI CUP 2023 competition. Dai et al focused specifically on LLM techniques (fine-tuning and ICL) in their competition setting, whereas our study employed both baseline models (rule-based and traditional ML techniques) and the LLM-based model (SFT and ICL). Our study crucially contributes to understanding the capabilities and limitations of current and emerging LLMs in the field of healthcare. Additionally, the detailed error analysis conducted in our study offers insights into specific factors contributing to FN occurrences, advancing our knowledge of difficulties in SHI identification. Jonnagaddala et al highlight the need to preserve privacy while using LLMs for de-identification. Our research incorporates some of these strategies to assess the performance and privacy-preserving properties of LLMs in deidentifying EHRs. Our results revealed notable performance variations across the five datasets, primarily driven by differences in documentation styles and SHI density. For instance, datasets with highly structured notes, such as MIMIC-2008, showed higher baseline stability, whereas the more diverse clinical narratives in i2b2-2014 posed greater challenges for zero-shot LLM configurations. Our error analysis further identified that specific SHI categories, particularly ‘dates’ and ‘provider names’, were more prone to FN in rule-based systems due to their highly variable formats. In contrast, LLM-based approaches—especially fine-tuned models—demonstrated a clear advantage in contextual understanding, successfully identifying ambiguous identifiers that rule-based patterns often missed. This suggests that while rule-based systems remain useful for standard formats, the integration of LLMs is essential for capturing context-dependent SHIs, thereby reducing the risk of residual identifiers in complex clinical narratives.

Moore et al introduced the Pyclipse framework, which harmonised the de-identification of SHI in clinical text data against the standardisation of the evaluation process. They used the parquet format, which deals with mapping SHI entity types from different annotations. Heavner et al emphasised that appropriate harmonisation must be accompanied by good documentation for quality checks and interpretations of results. They emphasised the need to carry out an amiable planning process and validate harmonisation to ensure the proper integrity and good usability of the data. There are several challenges and limitations in the harmonisation of datasets. One of them is specific types of data elements that are unavailable, which creates ambiguity and inconsistency in the harmonised dataset. Second, comprehensive manual verification of harmonisation, leading to errors.

Despite the considerable promise of LLMs, several specific challenges exist; variability in the structure and content of medical records can lead to inconsistencies in the performance of LLMs. Unlike casual conversational applications, where mistakes have a minimal impact, errors in the medical domain can have serious repercussions for patient care and outcomes. LLMs, which function as autoregressive language models, anticipate certain elements in a text, typically a word, by analysing preceding natural language texts. Once a mistaken prediction begins, whether it is a semantic error or a biased language usage, the model persists without self-correction, potentially propagating inaccuracies throughout the generated text. Moreover, LLMs trained on general language data may lack the domain-specific knowledge necessary for accurate de-identification in medical contexts. This can result in errors where the model fails to recognise context-specific identifiers, such as rare diseases or unique medical procedures, which may inadvertently be left identifiable.

Another significant challenge is the risk of re-identification, particularly considering the advancement of data mining and re-identification algorithms. This concern is particularly pronounced in the realm of large-scale clinical text and clinical LLMs, where the sensitive nature of the data and the limitations of automatic de-identification systems pose significant obstacles in achieving complete removal of SHIs. Furthermore, the presence of biases and hallucination in LLMs can inadvertently impact the effectiveness of de-identification processes in healthcare, potentially compromising patient privacy and the integrity of medical data. The high accuracy achieved by fine-tuned LLMs (eg, setting 9) suggests their readiness for integration into clinical pipelines. To visualise this, illustrates a typical workflow where the de-identification engine serves as a critical safety layer before performing downstream tasks such as medical notes summarisation. Finally, the ethical and legal implications of relying on automated systems for de-identification should be considered carefully. Errors in de-identification can lead to severe breaches of patient confidentiality and legal repercussions, making it imperative to continuously monitor and validate the performance of these models in real-world applications.

## Conclusion
This study provides a systematic benchmark of LLMs focused on identifying SHI in EHRs. LLMs are efficient in SHI de-identification tasks because they use natural language understanding of potentially sensitive information and automatically replace it with appropriate surrogate information. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of EHRs, varying degrees of biased output, and implementation challenges are some of the persistent problems. We believe that with appropriate privacy-preserving and implementation strategies and governance, LLMs can be incorporated into clinical workflows to handle SHI de-identification.
